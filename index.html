Study of Ventilators

1. Introduction & Theory

Mechanics & Artificial Ventilation

Respiration supplies oxygen to the tissues and removes carbon dioxide from them[cite: 1]. Inspiration results from the contraction of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, enlarging the thoracic cavity[cite: 1]. In respiratory failure, mechanical ventilators supply enough oxygen and remove the right amount of CO₂, keeping arterial partial pressures at desired levels[cite: 1]. Positive-pressure ventilators apply a pressure greater than atmospheric to the airway to inflate the lungs[cite: 2].

Parameters & Modes

Minute Volume (VE) is calculated as VE = VT × f, where VT is Tidal Volume and f is respiration rate[cite: 2].

Common modes include CMV (machine controls tidal volume and rate), SIMV (mandatory breath is timed to patient's own effort), and CPAP (holds a constant positive pressure while the patient breathes at will)[cite: 2, 3]. Modern systems use microprocessors to blend oxygen and air, compute inspiratory flow trajectories, and trigger safety limits[cite: 5].

Tonometry & Arterial Pulse Waveform

1. Introduction & Theory

Principle of Tonometry

Tonometry measures pressure by sensing the force required to flatten or applanate a surface[cite: 8]. In arterial tonometry, a sensitive pressure transducer is applied over a superficial artery, such as the carotid, to record pulsatile pressure variations[cite: 8]. The pressure fluctuations are converted into proportional electrical signals for visualization[cite: 8].

Waveform Characteristics

The arterial pulse waveform corresponds to cardiovascular mechanical activity. It contains a rapid upstroke associated with systolic ejection, a dicrotic notch indicating the closure of the aortic valve, and a gradual downstroke corresponding to diastolic relaxation[cite: 8]. Variations indicate physiological or pathological changes[cite: 8].

Working of Defibrillator

1. Introduction & Theory

Ventricular Fibrillation

Ventricular fibrillation is a serious cardiac emergency resulting from the asynchronous contraction of heart muscle[cite: 9]. This uncoordinated movement leads to loss of cardiac output and irreversible damage if not reversed within 5 minutes[cite: 9].

Defibrillation Principle

A high energy shock causes all muscle fibers to contract simultaneously, allowing them to respond to normal intrinsic pulses[cite: 9]. The circuit utilizes a variable autotransformer, a rectifier, and an oil-filled capacitor to store and discharge energy (programmable in Joules) across the heart[cite: 9]. Monophasic and biphasic pulses are used to restore normal sinus rhythm[cite: 9].
